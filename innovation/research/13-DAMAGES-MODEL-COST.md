# What a wage-and-hour damages model actually costs a firm

Research for `buildouts/01-backpay/PLAN.md` §4, which currently marks
**"Hours saved per matter: [NOT ESTABLISHED]"** and proposes to settle it by
asking five firms. It does not need asking. Plaintiff firms itemise their hours
in fee motions, under penalty of perjury, and California courts publish rulings
that quote those hours back and sometimes cut them. This file works from those.

Written 2026-09-01.

**Payback is not the hard part.** The arithmetic clears by a factor of four even
when you assume the tool only does 40% of the job. The hard part is that $2,300
of paralegal salary is not a number a partner feels, and on contingency those
hours are lodestar the firm wants to *have*. §7 argues that against myself.

## 1. Paralegal billing rates in California

**Answer: $150 to $300 per hour for plaintiff-side employment work, with the
defensible centre around $225. Courts in expensive counties approve the top of
that band; courts outside them cut it.**

Market surveys, which courts cite as corroboration:

- **$214/hr** — average California non-lawyer (paralegal and other non-licensed
  staff) billing rate, Clio 2025 Legal Trends data. National average $187.
  [review]
- **$116–$314** — spread of non-lawyer rates by practice area. [review]
- The **US Consumer Law Attorney Fee Survey Report** (Burdge) publishes
  California paralegal medians by metro, including Los Angeles–Long Beach–Anaheim,

  exist. [NOT ESTABLISHED] as to the actual numbers. If someone can open that

**The cash side, which is a different number entirely.** California paralegal
median pay is **$35.08/hour** ($72,960/year), BLS May 2024, across 45,740
people in the state. [review] Load that with payroll tax, benefits and overhead
at 1.4× and the firm's actual outlay is roughly **$49/hour**. The billing rate
is four to six times the cash cost. Hold that thought until §6.

## 3. Hours attributable to damages analysis specifically

This is the question the plan actually needs and it is the one the record
answers worst. Here is what is genuinely there.

### 3a. The one clean number

**Notre Dame de Namur, Case No. 19-CIV-04765** (Declaration of Julian Hammond,
San Mateo County; 397 class members):

> Plaintiffs' Counsel expended **156.1 hours** analysing class data and
> preparing a detailed mediation brief and reviewing Defendant's brief, for a
> lodestar of **$96,521**. [review]

That is $618/hour blended and it is the closest thing to a damages line item I
could find in a California wage-and-hour matter. Two caveats, both real:

1. **It is a combined phase.** Data analysis and brief-writing are billed
   together. How much is modelling and how much is advocacy prose is not
   recoverable from the document.
2. The same declaration reportedly shows **69.1 hours** on preliminary and final
   approval motions plus notice administration to those 397 members, and a
   22-hour mediation-attendance entry with a $14,874 lodestar. That third figure
   implies $676/hour, which the search summary attributes to paralegals. That
   cannot be a paralegal rate. **The attribution is garbled and I am not using
   that row.** [conflict]

### 3b. Total hours per matter, for scale

| Hours | Matter |
|---:|---|
| 125.1 | Lodestar $83,965 [review] |
| 160.5 | Two attorneys, 130.60 + 26.90 + 3 anticipated, Santa Barbara [review] |
| 239.1 | Lodestar $97,740, Fresno [review] |
| 320.6 | **Ameripec**, all class counsel: Han 82.8, Tatavos-Gharajeh 72.6, Rothman 165.2; fee awarded $157,500 [review] |
| 380.1 | Lodestar $243,905 [review] |
| 395.9 | Five attorneys [review] |
| **1,565.7** | **Dixon v. Cushman & Wakefield Western**, 3:18-cv-05813-JSC and two consolidated cases, through Oct 2021; $4.9m for 476 people [review] |

The Cushman case is the only one with a published phase breakdown, and it is
phase, not task: **166.2 hours** investigating and pleading, **687.2 hours** on
discovery, **345.4 hours** on the settlement and notice phase. [review] Those
three sum to 1,198.8 of 1,565.7, leaving roughly 367 hours unaccounted across
certification, motion practice and mediation. **No damages line.**

### 3c. So what is the honest number

Two routes, and they agree, which is the only reason I am willing to put a
figure on it at all.

**Route one, top-down.** An ordinary single-defendant California wage-and-hour
class or PAGA matter runs **125–400 hours** end to end. If class-data work and
damages modelling is 15–25% of that, it is **19–100 hours**, centre ~40.

**Route two, from the phase.** The Notre Dame de Namur data-and-brief phase is
156.1 hours for a 397-member class. If damages modelling is a third to a half of
that phase, it is **52–78 hours**.

Overlap: **25–80 hours, centre 45.** [derived, and it is an estimate, not a
finding]

**What the record does not give, stated plainly:** not one fee declaration I
reached breaks out "damages model" as a billing category with its own hour
count. Fee applications are organised by litigation phase, because that is what
courts want to see. Anyone claiming a precise damages-hours figure from public
filings is reading something into them that is not there.

### 3d. What the work consists of

From practitioner sources, which describe the task even where they do not price
it:

- Foundational inputs are **class size, workweeks and pay periods** across the
  damages period, plus policies, wage statements and a **representative sample**
  of time records. [review]
- Counsel "created violation, damages, and penalties valuations and models" and
  extrapolated a per-violation count to the whole class. [review]
- The damages period is **three years** for the wage claims, **four** where the
  UCL is pleaded for restitution, which changes the workweek count by a third.
  [review]
- Where a sample is used to prove class-wide damages, **an expert declaration is
  required** to defend the sampling method. [review]

## 4. What a damages expert costs instead

**Rates, published:**

- **SEAK 2024 Survey of Expert Witness Fees**, ~1,600 experts across 250+
  specialties: median **$450/hr** file review and preparation, **$475/hr**
  deposition, **$500/hr** testimony. [review]
- Economic and financial experts specifically: around **$425/hr** for analysis
  and **$525/hr** for testimony; forensic accountants in complex matters
  **$500–$700/hr**. [review]
- One economic consultancy states the general band for economic damages experts
  as **$300 to $800+/hr**. [review]

**Engagement size, which is the surprise.** Cost declarations in California
wage-and-hour settlements itemise expert fees, and the amounts are small:

- **$4,880** expert fees in one settlement, against $15,000 of mediation fees
  and $1,660 of filing fees. [review]
- In another, **expert fees, filing fees, printing and attorney service together
  totalled $5,597.78**, with mediation at $9,450 being the largest single cost.
  [review]

At $450/hour, $4,880 buys about **eleven hours**. So the typical California
wage-and-hour expert engagement at the mediation stage is not a full Rule 26
report with deposition. It is a consultant taking the produced data, running an
exposure model, and handing back a number the mediator will accept.
**$3,000–$10,000 is the realistic band**, and it is far below what the hourly
rates alone would suggest. That is a genuinely useful finding and it cuts against
the tool's positioning: the expert alternative is cheaper than expected.

## 5. How the payroll data actually arrives

The mechanics matter because they determine whether an automated tool can eat
the input at all.

**Class sizes, real examples:**

| Class | Matter |
|---:|---|
| 397 | Notre Dame de Namur [review] |
| 476 | Dixon v. Cushman & Wakefield ($4.9m) [review] |
| **2,593** | Metzke v. TFSI, Case No. CGC-19-581914 (San Francisco): 2,593 California non-exempt employees, 660 of them former, across **173,434 workweeks**, average hourly rate **$27.15** [review] |
| 13,775 | Sephora ($12.75m) [review] |
| 16,577 | Frausto v. Bank of America, 3:18-cv-01983-LB ($1.89m) [review] |

**Format, from a forensic accountant who does this work:**

> Wage-and-hour class action engagements typically involve assembling daily
> hours worked for each class member from timesheet reports, **which often are
> not in machine-readable format and must be input manually** into a summary
> spreadsheet. The same is true of employee wage information from payroll
> reports. [review — Thomas Neches & Company, engagement description]

Corroborated by damages-consulting firms describing the same problem: historical
records arrive as **spreadsheets, delimited files, native PDFs or scanned PDFs**;
employers switch payroll providers and timekeeping platforms mid-period; the
same employee carries different identifiers in different systems; work periods
and pay periods do not align. A single matter can involve **millions of payroll
transactions**. Scanned handwritten timecards have to be validated against pay
period totals. [review]

One production was described as **detailed payroll records for the entire class
covering over 350,000 shifts**. [review — case not identified; I could not tie
this figure to a named docket and am reporting it as an unattributed quantum.]

**Pre-mediation, the production is usually a sample, not the full class.** A
**Belaire-West** administration produces the contactable class list; the damages
data before mediation is typically a representative sample of time and wage
records with class-wide aggregates (workweeks, pay periods, headcount). Parties
sometimes stipulate not to challenge the sample's representativeness. [review]

**Two consequences for the product, and they point in opposite directions.**
Scanned PDFs and manual re-keying are exactly the pain a machine removes, which
is the bull case. But if the pre-mediation production is a stipulated sample
with agreed aggregates rather than a full class-wide extract, the arithmetic on
top of it is simpler than the full-class case and there is less to automate. The
plan should not assume every matter arrives with 173,434 workweeks of punch data.

## 6. The arithmetic

**Inputs**, all carried from above:

- Hours per damages model: **25–80, centre 45** [derived, low confidence]
- Paralegal billing rate: **$225** [review, centre of $150–$300]
- Associate: **$500**. Partner: **$750**. [review]
- Paralegal loaded salary cost: **$49/hr** [derived from BLS $35.08 × 1.4]
- Subscription: **$299/month = $3,588/year**

### The mixed-team lodestar cost of one model

45 hours split 60% paralegal, 30% associate, 10% partner:

| Timekeeper | Hours | Rate | Value |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Paralegal | 27.0 | $225 | $6,075 |
| Associate | 13.5 | $500 | $6,750 |
| Partner | 4.5 | $750 | $3,375 |
| **Total** | **45.0** | **$360 blended** | **$16,200** |

Sanity check against the one real phase: Notre Dame de Namur blended $618/hour
across 156.1 hours. My $360 blended is lower because I have weighted toward
paralegal work, which is what the plan assumes. If the real mix is more
attorney-heavy, the lodestar figure goes **up**, not down, and the payback case
gets stronger. The estimate is conservative in the right direction.

### The cash cost of the same 45 hours

45 × $49 = **$2,205**. Round to **$2,300**.

Range across the hour band: 25 × $46 = **$1,150** to 80 × $53 = **$4,240**.

### Payback, three bases

$299/month ÷ saving per matter = matters per month the tool must touch.

| Basis | Saving per matter | Matters/month needed | Equivalent |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| **Cash cost**, full substitution | $2,250 | **0.13** | one matter every **7.5 months** |
| **Expert substitution** | $5,000 | **0.06** | one every **16.7 months** |
| **Lodestar value**, full substitution | $16,200 | **0.018** | one every **54 months** |
| **Cash, 40% substitution** | $900 | **0.33** | one matter **per quarter** |
| **Lodestar, 40% substitution** | $6,480 | **0.046** | one every **21.7 months** |

Annualised: at $2,250 saved per matter the subscription needs **1.6 matters a
year**. At a pessimistic $900 it needs **4.0 matters a year**.

**Is that a low bar or a high one? Low.** A California firm that takes
wage-and-hour class or PAGA work at all is carrying more than four such matters
a year; the fee declarations above come from firms running many simultaneously.
The threshold is cleared by a firm doing this work occasionally, let alone one
doing it as its practice.

**The number that should be quoted to a firm is the cash one, $2,250, not the
lodestar one.** $16,200 is the value of the time, and quoting it is the sort of
inflation this project is supposed to refuse. A partner hears "you'll save
$16,000 a matter", checks it against a paralegal salary, and stops trusting
everything else on the page.

## 7. The argument against my own conclusion

Three objections, and the first is serious.

**One: on contingency, those hours are an asset.** Class counsel's fee motion
lives or dies on lodestar. Hours removed from the damages model are lodestar
removed from the cross-check, and the fee requested is often justified as a
*negative* multiplier on lodestar — 0.93 in Cushman & Wakefield, under 0.27 in
Frausto. [review] A firm whose fee request is already comfortably below its
lodestar loses nothing. A firm relying on lodestar to justify a fee above the
25% benchmark has a live reason not to want fewer hours. This is a real
counter-incentive and the plan does not currently mention it.

**Two: $2,300 of salaried paralegal time is not felt as a cost.** The paralegal
is on payroll either way. There is no invoice. The saving is capacity, which is
only worth money if the freed capacity gets used, and at a small plaintiff firm
it usually does. But the buying conversation is harder than a $2,250 line item
suggests.

**Three: the expert alternative is cheaper than expected.** $4,880 buys a
credible exposure model with a declaration behind it. A $299/month tool competes
with that on speed and on the marginal matter, not on price.

**Where the value actually sits**, if the payback framing is wrong: the tool
moves work off the **partner's** desk, not the paralegal's. Partner time in the
Notre Dame de Namur phase implies $618/hour blended. If even five of the 45
hours are partner hours at $750, that is $3,750 of the most constrained resource
in a small firm. Speed at mediation, and a number that survives the mediator
asking how it was derived, are worth more than the paralegal's Tuesday.

## 8. What is still not established

- **The Burdge survey's California paralegal medians.** Free, published,
  court-cited, and I could not open the PDF. Highest-value single gap.
- **Any fee declaration that breaks out damages modelling as its own billing
  category with hours.** I did not find one. It may not exist, because fee
  applications are structured by phase.
- **The 45-hour centre.** It is an inference from one combined phase and seven
  matter totals. Two or three more phase-level breakdowns would move it from
  low to medium confidence.
- **The split of the 156.1-hour Notre Dame de Namur phase** between data
  analysis and brief-writing. Not recoverable from what I could see.
- **Whether the pre-mediation production is typically a sample or the full class
  extract**, and in what proportion. This decides how much arithmetic the tool
  is actually replacing.

## 9. What this changes in the plan

§4 should stop saying "ask five firms". Replace the `[NOT ESTABLISHED]` line
with:

> **Hours per damages model: 25–80, centre ~45**, from California fee
> declarations and superior court rulings on lodestar. Cash cost to the firm
> ≈ **$2,300** at loaded paralegal salary; lodestar value ≈ **$16,000**.
> A $299/month subscription pays for itself at **1.6 matters a year** on the
> cash basis, or **4 matters a year** if the tool only removes 40% of the work.
> Confidence: medium-high on rates, low on the hour centre. Sources: §13 of
> research.

And add the counter-incentive, because it is the thing a firm will say back:
**lodestar is an asset in a fee motion, and hours removed are lodestar removed.**
The answer to it is that most California wage-and-hour fee requests already run
at a negative multiplier, so the lodestar is not the binding constraint. That is
an answer worth having ready rather than discovering on a call.
