# 6 — The 2am line

**97% of dementia caregivers say they want navigation help. The crisis happens at night, and the helpline is shut.**

`PROVEN need, BET on delivery` · Buyer: the family caregiver · Form: voice subscription

---

## The gap

The evidence here is the strongest need-evidence in this entire project, because
the caregivers were asked directly and answered.

| Figure | Class |
|---|---|
| US family caregivers for dementia | **11.5 million**, providing **18.4 billion hours** unpaid [review] |
| Per caregiver | **~31 hours/week**, **1,612 hours/year** [review] |
| Value of that unpaid care | **~$350 billion/year** [review] |
| Out-of-pocket cost borne by the caregiver | **$12,388/year** average [review] |
| Depression prevalence in dementia caregivers | **up to 40%** [review] |
| Say coordinating care is stressful | **70%** [review] |
| Have difficulty finding resources and support | **66%** [review] |
| **Would find navigation services helpful** | **97%** [review] |

Then the request, in their own ranking. The most-cited services caregivers want:
**a 24/7 helpline (36%)**, help coordinating between specialists (34%), and help
understanding the condition (34%) [review]. Among their top ten stated needs:
**communication techniques with the person (16%)** and **managing neuropsychiatric
symptoms and behaviours (10%)** [review].

**A 97% "yes" to a direct question about whether you want this is about as close
to proven demand as consumer research ever gets.**

## Why the night specifically

Sundowning — escalating confusion and agitation in the late afternoon and evening
— affects somewhere between **2.5% and 66%** of people with dementia depending on
how it is measured [review]. The research describes its effect on the carer as
*"physical exhaustion from managing disruptive behaviours during evening hours
when they themselves are fatigued... disrupted sleep... chronic sleep
deprivation"* [review].

So the hardest moment of the day is the moment when every support service is
closed, the doctor's office is shut, and the caregiver is most depleted. That is
not a gap in a market. That is a hole in the shape of a product.

## What it is

**A phone number that is always answered, by something that knows this person.**

- **Voice-first, because it is 2am** and the caregiver is standing in a hallway
  with someone who is frightened. Not an app. Not typing.
- **It knows the specific person** — their stage, their history, what worked last
  Tuesday, that they respond to their daughter's name, that the agitation follows
  the diuretic. This is what makes it different from a generic helpline, and it is
  only possible because it is the same number every night.
- **Techniques, in the moment.** The evidence-based approaches to redirection and
  de-escalation are documented and teachable, and 16% of caregivers name
  communication technique as a top need. A calm voice saying "don't correct her —
  agree, then redirect to the garden" at the moment it is needed is the product.
- **It logs the episode**, so the pattern the caregiver is too tired to see gets
  seen, and so the neurologist gets a real history at the next appointment instead
  of "she's been a bit worse."
- **Hard escalation rules.** Falls, injury, wandering, aspiration, chest pain,
  medication error — these go to emergency services or the on-call clinician
  immediately and without negotiation.

## The innovation

The existing money in this space — **Rippl ($23M Series A), Craniometrix ($7.5M),
Harmonic Health** — is going into clinical care navigation delivered through
Medicare's GUIDE programme, sold to payers and health systems [review]. That is a
real and worthy business and it is **not this one.** It operates in business
hours, it serves the patient, and it reaches the family through a clinical
channel.

**Nobody is selling the family a 2am voice that knows their mother.** The gap is
between what the clinical system provides and what the 36% asking for a 24/7 line
actually mean.

## Money

| Price | To reach $30k/mo |
|---|---|
| $39/mo | **770 families** |
| $49/mo | **613 families** |
| $79/mo | **380 families** |

Against **$12,388** a year already spent out-of-pocket per caregiver, $49/month
is 4.7% of what they are already paying. **613 families out of 11.5 million
caregivers** is a share too small to be a market-size question.

## Risks — and these are serious

- **This is a bet, and it is labelled one.** The need is proven; that a voice
  product satisfies it is not. A caregiver in genuine crisis may find an AI voice
  enraging rather than comforting, and I have no evidence either way.
- **Safety is the whole game.** Every failure mode is a vulnerable person. The
  escalation rules are the product's spine, not a feature — and the FTC's
  DoNotPay order is the precedent for what happens when an AI service is marketed
  as competent at something it was never measured at.
- **Medical advice is a bright line.** Behavioural technique is not medical
  advice; medication questions are. The line must be drawn hard and never crossed.
- **The buyer is exhausted and broke.** Acquisition through Alzheimer's
  associations, support groups and geriatric care managers is more plausible than
  direct marketing.
- Grief-adjacent churn: the subscription ends when the person dies, and average
  hold time may be short.

## Where to start — no phone calls required

**The published record here is unusually rich, and it should be read before
anything is built.** There is a qualitative analysis of national dementia helpline
calls [review] — an actual corpus of what caregivers ring up and ask. Read it and
build the response library from the real distribution of questions, not from
imagination.

Then the measurement that decides it: have **geriatric care specialists grade 100
simulated 2am exchanges** for safety and quality. If the escalation rules do not
fire correctly every single time in that set, it does not ship.
