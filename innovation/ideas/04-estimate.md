# 4 — The second opinion

**77% of drivers think they're being ripped off. None of them can prove it in the waiting room.**

`PROVEN pain, CONTESTED market` · Buyer: the driver · Form: consumer app, per-use or thin subscription

---

## The gap

| Figure | Class |
|---|---|
| US drivers who distrust auto repair shops | **77%** [review — AAA consumer survey] |
| Parts markup, independent shops | **25–60%** over wholesale [review] |
| Parts markup, dealerships | **40–80%** [review] |
| Repair cost increase, early 2022 → June 2024 | **~25%** [review] |
| Drivers delaying repairs over cost | **82%** [review] |

The AAA figure is the striking one: **77%, and "that number hasn't meaningfully
moved in years, and the repair industry hasn't solved it"** [review].

Three-quarters of a country distrusting an entire industry is not a customer
service problem. It is an information asymmetry so complete that the customer
cannot evaluate the product even after buying it. You are told you need a part
you cannot see, for a reason you cannot verify, at a price you cannot compare,
by the only person who knows.

And the recommended defence, per the research, is almost comic in its inadequacy:
**"a written, itemized estimate reviewed line by line before you authorize any
work"** [review]. Reviewed by whom? The person who doesn't know what a control
arm costs.

## What it is

Photograph the estimate. Get an answer before you say yes.

- **Line-by-line reading of the quote**: what each item is, in plain words, and
  whether the labour hours match published flat-rate guides for that job on that
  vehicle.
- **Part price against actual retail.** The single most checkable form of
  overcharging, and the one nobody checks.
- **"Does this job actually require this part?"** — the diagnostic question.
  Bundled work that isn't implied by the symptom is the most common upsell and is
  visible from the estimate alone.
- **What to say.** Not a rant — three specific, non-confrontational questions that
  demonstrate to the shop that the customer is being advised. In a market built on
  asymmetry, visibly removing the asymmetry changes the price.
- **Optionally: what a fair total looks like** for that job in that postcode.

## The innovation

The industry's answer to distrust has been to certify shops — networks, badges,
warranties — which asks the customer to trust a different institution. **This
gives the customer their own competence instead.** It is a translator, not a
referee, and it works in the waiting room in ninety seconds, which is the only
moment when the decision is actually being made.

Note that this generalises directly: roofing, HVAC, plumbing, dentistry, vet
care. Auto is the wedge because the estimate is a standardised document and the
labour times are published.

## Money

| Model | To reach $30k/mo |
|---|---|
| $29 per check | **12,414/yr — 1,035 a month** |
| $9/mo subscription | **3,334 subscribers** |

**This is the weakest money in the set and it should be read that way.** Consumer
transactional pricing needs volume, and volume needs marketing spend. The two
better routes:

1. **Insurers and roadside clubs bundle it** as a member benefit. One deal
   replaces all the marketing. AAA itself published the distrust statistic.
2. **Extended warranty providers**, whose costs go up when shops overcharge.

## Risks

- **The most competitive space in this document.** The press release found during
  research — *"AAA: 77% of Drivers Fear Mechanic Overcharges. One Service Fixes
  That"* — is someone already selling into this exact statistic. RepairPal,
  Openbay and similar have existed for a decade.
- **Being wrong is expensive for the user.** Telling someone a needed repair is
  unnecessary can put them in a broken car or an unsafe one. Brake and steering
  advice must be conservative by design.
- **Shops will fight it**, and shops are the distribution channel for several
  adjacent products.
- Labour time guides are commercial, licensed data. That is a real per-customer
  cost and possibly a blocker.

## Where to start — no phone calls required

**Collect fifty real repair estimates** — they are posted constantly in public car
forums and consumer complaint threads — and grade the model against a mechanic's
review of the same fifty. Two numbers decide it: **how often it correctly flags an
inflated line, and how often it wrongly flags a legitimate one.**

The second number is the one that matters. A tool that cries wolf at an honest
shop is worse than nothing, and it is the failure mode that ends the business.
